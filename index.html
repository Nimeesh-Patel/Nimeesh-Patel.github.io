1Interests

Mostly epistemology: how knowledge grows, and what creativity, morality and interest have to do with it. I follow Karl Popper and David Deutsch, and Thomas Babington Macaulay in politics.

From there it spreads out: what general intelligence is; the Enlightenment, and why some societies keep making progress while others stay static; physics through constructor theory and the multiverse; and beauty, which for me mostly means music, with film and art close behind. Also chess.

I try to follow what interests me rather than set distant goals. A destination chosen in advance makes a mistake hard to discover; what I want now, criticised by the long term, doesn't. Right now I want:

  • a more progressive, critical culture around me than I have;
  • a wider audience for ideas, through a platform or an institution;
  • to earn by solving problems I find fun, until I'm free to give full interest to the problems I love;
  • to understand general intelligence well enough that it could be built;
  • an institution for the sustained creation of knowledge.

3Conjectures

Tap a card to turn it over. These are tentative, and some have open edges I haven't solved.

3.1What is the difference between a normal program and a general intelligence?

A normal program can be defined by its specification: its outputs for given inputs. A general intelligence can't, because its morality isn't fixed. It can change the criteria by which it judges ideas, and changing one's morality is what disobedience means. A GI doesn't even need input; it can just think.

GIs are better than any other program at guessing and criticising because they understand the problem: they disobey the current solutions, guess new ones meaning to solve it, and criticise the guesses against it. Since there's no fixed mapping from inputs to outputs, no benchmark can test for general intelligence.

LLMs don't have problems of their own. What they do is perspiration: drawing out the implications of knowledge that already exists. That makes them very good at connecting known dots and unable to create new ones — a difference in kind, not degree.

↑ 2.1
3.2How are morality, disobedience and creativity related?

Morality is the problem of what to do next: of where to direct creativity. Knowledge comes via creativity, and creativity via disobedience — not following the existing ideas or criteria about a problem you find interesting. Disobedience has to be about a problem; without one it's just randomness.

Interest is how problems get selected, so it's partly moral. Fun is when no idea in the mind is taking authority over another, which makes it a form of disobedience — and why "boring now, rewarding later" patience is really obedience.

Open edge: creativity needs disobedience, disobedience is a moral choice that needs moral knowledge, and knowledge needs creativity. This looks circular, and I haven't resolved it.

↑ 2.2
3.3Why do observations require disobedience?

Observations aren't possible without disobedience. An observation corrects knowledge only by clashing with a theory, and admitting the clash means disobeying the theory.

Corollary: animals, and any program other than a general intelligence, can't have observations.

I'd thought about this problem for a long time, and this idea surprised me. It could be completely wrong, but it seems to solve the problem and fits the rest of my theory of morality.

↑ 2.3
3.4How is beauty related to knowledge?

Beauty draws interest. It makes you dwell on something and ask "how is this possible?" — perhaps it's what an inconsistency between theories feels like when it pulls you towards a problem. Clarity is beautiful because it carries a good explanation.

Bees aren't universal explainers, yet they are drawn to objective beauty they weren't specifically programmed to recognise. If beauty works like that on people too, it can draw someone towards epistemology even before they have the problems that would lead them there.

↑ 2.4
3.5How can the Enlightenment be progressed?

A dynamic society is one where rational memes have the advantage over anti-rational ones, because of its tradition of criticism, its optimism and its embrace of creativity. Anti-rational memes survive by disabling the critical faculties of the people who hold them; the problem isn't people but the ideas in their minds.

All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge, so the speed of progress may be the only real defence. My conjecture for speeding it up is a research institution on a capitalist model: people and companies support it for the knowledge it creates and can withdraw whenever they like, which keeps it exposed to criticism. It has to be free; only free people create a lot of knowledge.

↑ 2.5
3.6How can I keep what I think so I can come back and build on it?

Store it problem-first: one problem per note, question above the line and conjecture below, like this page. Attention follows problems, so a note stays findable only if a live problem points to it. Resurface old problems with spaced repetition so they meet new ideas.
